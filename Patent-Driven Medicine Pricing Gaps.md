# Patent-Driven Medicine Pricing Gaps: Documented Cases & Existing Open Alternatives

*Compiled July 2026*

## Scope, method, and one important boundary

This document catalogues well-documented cases where patents — or patent-adjacent tactics like evergreening, device patents, and regulatory-exclusivity capture — have kept a medicine priced out of reach for a meaningful population, and pairs each with the **existing, legitimate, patent-free pathway** that already provides (or is emerging to provide) the same therapeutic benefit: generics, biosimilars, older off-patent drug classes, voluntary/compulsory licensing, or genuine nonprofit and open-source pharma R&D.

**What "alternative" means here, deliberately:** every alternative below is something that already exists in the world — approved by a regulator, licensed by a patent-holder, produced by a nonprofit consortium, or otherwise real and checkable. Nothing here is a novel compound I've designed. That's a firm line for two reasons: drug safety and efficacy require clinical trial validation that no amount of literature synthesis substitutes for, and presenting an untested molecule as a "medical alternative" could genuinely hurt someone. Where I mention DIY/biohacker synthesis efforts (there's one section on this), I describe that they exist and why experts are worried about them — I don't reproduce or link their protocols.

**One fairness note:** patents on medicines exist for a real reason — drug development is expensive, slow, and failure-prone, and patent-driven exclusivity is the main mechanism developed economies use to fund it. Every case below involves a *specific, documented* tactic (not just "having a patent") that a court, regulator, or investigative body has identified as going beyond that ordinary incentive function. I've tried to represent the pricing rationale where the manufacturer offered one.

This isn't literally exhaustive — no single document can be given hundreds of medicines have faced some version of this dynamic. It covers 16 major, heavily-documented cases spanning the distinct mechanisms in play, plus the nine main structural responses (legal, nonprofit, and grassroots) currently being used to close these gaps, plus an honest accounting of where no good alternative yet exists.

## Part 1 — Documented cases

### 1. Analog insulins (Humalog, NovoLog, Lantus) — diabetes

**The gap.** Insulin was discovered in 1923; its co-discoverers sold the original patent to the University of Toronto for $1, explicitly so it would remain universally affordable. A century later, "analog" insulins — chemically tweaked, faster- or longer-acting versions — have followed a very different path. Humalog launched in 1996 at roughly $21/vial and by 2017 listed around $275, an increase far outpacing any change in the underlying formula. NovoLog followed a near-identical trajectory, from about $40 to roughly $290 by 2018. A National Library of Medicine analysis found 63% of patents on studied insulin products relate to *delivery devices* — pens and injectors — and 85% of those device patents don't even mention insulin in their claims; device patents alone extended market protection by a median of 5.2 years beyond the underlying compound patents. Sanofi alone filed 74 patent applications around Lantus, creating what patent-transparency group I-MAK calculated as the potential for a 37-year effective monopoly on one product.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Older "human" insulins (Regular and NPH, sold under names like Humulin and, at lower retail cost through programs like Walmart's ReliOn line) are off-patent, still FDA-approved, and still effective — the tradeoff is less flexible timing than analogs, not reduced efficacy for most patients. Separately, the first interchangeable insulin biosimilar (insulin glargine-yfgn, marketed as Semglee) was approved in 2021, the biosimilar pathway starting to do for insulin what it did for other biologics.

**Status: Partially resolved.** Older insulins and one interchangeable biosimilar exist; newer analogs (e.g., Tresiba) remain patent-protected into the 2030s.

*Sources: [LegalClarity](https://legalclarity.org/when-does-the-insulin-patent-expire/), [T1International/I-MAK](https://www.t1international.com/blog/2019/01/20/why-insulin-so-expensive/), [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2018/12/07/patent-abuse-rising-drug-prices-lantus/)*

### 2. EpiPen — anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction)

**The gap.** Epinephrine itself is a century-old, essentially free-to-manufacture drug — about $1 worth per dose. What's patented is the auto-injector *device*. Mylan acquired EpiPen in 2007 and raised its price from roughly $100 to over $600 for a two-pack by 2016, while the drug cost inside remained unchanged. Congressional hearings and an FTC-adjacent price-gouging lawsuit followed; Mylan and Pfizer ultimately paid $264 million and $345 million respectively in 2021–2022 settlements. Mylan released its own "authorized generic" in December 2016 at $300 — an unusual case of a company competing with itself — but by 2018, brand EpiPen still listed around $700 and even the generic had crept to about $400 for a two-pack.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Generic epinephrine auto-injectors (Mylan's own authorized generic, and Teva's true generic approved in 2018) and Impax's Adrenaclick generic (around $110) both cost meaningfully less. A cheaper but less convenient option — plain epinephrine drawn from a vial with a syringe — is also off-patent and inexpensive, though it genuinely does require more training to administer correctly and quickly in an emergency, which is a real, not cosmetic, tradeoff for laypeople.

**Status: Partially resolved.** Generic competition exists and has taken real market share, but prices for all options crept back up over time — device patents, not the drug, remain the structural issue until 2025.

*Sources: [Pew Charitable Trusts](https://www.pew.org/en/research-and-analysis/articles/2016/09/22/beyond-epipen-prices-of-lifesaving-epinephrine-products-soar), [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/264-million-settlement-in-epipen-price-gouging-litigation), [GoodRx](https://www.goodrx.com/epinephrine-epipen/epipen-price-change-since-mylan-released-generic-epinephrine)*

### 3. Daraprim — toxoplasmosis

**The gap.** Pyrimethamine (Daraprim) is not actually patent-protected — it dates to 1953 and had been off-patent for decades, selling for about $13.50/pill. When Turing Pharmaceuticals (Martin Shkreli) acquired it in 2015, the price jumped overnight to $750/pill, a roughly 5,000% increase. This is an important edge case for this whole exercise: the barrier here was a *closed-distribution monopoly* in a market too small to attract generic competitors, not a patent at all — proof that patents aren't the only mechanism that can strand a medicine.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Compounding pharmacy Imprimis responded within weeks with a compounded pyrimethamine-plus-leucovorin formulation at about $1/pill ($99 for a 100-count bottle) — legal because compounding combines already-approved ingredients into a patient-specific prescription rather than copying Daraprim itself.

**Status: Resolved, via a workaround rather than the underlying market fix.** Generic pyrimethamine competition has also since emerged.

*Sources: [BioPharma Dive](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/san-diego-based-compounder-to-offer-1-alternative-to-turings-daraprim/407843/), [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/davidkroll/2015/10/23/imiprimis-ceo-on-compounding-a-low-cost-alternative-to-turings-daraprim-for-toxoplasmosis/)*

### 4. Sovaldi / Harvoni (sofosbuvir) — Hepatitis C

**The gap.** Gilead's Sovaldi, an actual cure for Hepatitis C, launched in 2013–2014 at roughly $84,000–$88,000 for a 12-week US course (~$1,000/pill), despite raw active-ingredient costs that fell to a few dollars per kilogram within a year of launch as generic manufacturing scaled in India.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Gilead voluntarily licensed generic manufacture to Indian companies in September 2014, covering 91 countries; a 2025 analysis found the generic sofosbuvir/daclatasvir price had fallen from roughly $14,925 per course at launch to about $40 by 2024 in licensed markets. The catch, documented by MSF's hepatitis coalition: the license explicitly excludes middle-income countries containing 46% of the global Hepatitis C population, who get none of this benefit.

**Status: Largely resolved for licensed low-income countries; unresolved for excluded middle-income countries and for US list pricing**, where the branded product remains extremely expensive absent insurance negotiation.

*Sources: [ResearchGate/UNITAID-funded study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289712197_Rapid_reductions_in_prices_for_generic_sofosbuvir_and_daclatasvir_to_treat_hepatitis_C), [hepCoalition](https://www.hepcoalition.org/en/advocate/campaigns/liberate-generics/article/gilead-s-license-on-hepatitis-c-drugs-sofosbuvir-and-ledipasvir-a-fool-s-594)*

### 5. Humira (adalimumab) — rheumatoid arthritis, Crohn's, psoriasis

**The gap.** Humira became the best-selling drug in pharmaceutical history — $20.4 billion in 2020 sales alone. Its core patent expired in 2016, but AbbVie built what a peer-reviewed Journal of Law and the Biosciences study called a patent thicket: over 100 secondary patents (some counts run to 132) on formulation, dosing regimen, and manufacturing process, derived from just eight original patent families. Biosimilar makers ultimately settled rather than litigate the whole thicket, agreeing not to enter the US market until 2023 in exchange for AbbVie not contesting their entry into Europe in 2018 — a five-year gap between the same biosimilar being available in the EU and the US for the same molecule.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Multiple adalimumab biosimilars (Amjevita, Cyltezo, Hyrimoz, and others) launched in the US starting 2023.

**Status: Resolved, but demonstrates how effective a patent thicket can be at delaying an outcome that eventually happens anyway** — US patients paid full price for roughly five extra years compared to Europeans.

*Sources: [Oxford Journal of Law and the Biosciences](https://academic.oup.com/jlb/article/9/2/lsac022/6680093), [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/humira-the-first-20-billion-drug), [Mintz](https://www.mintz.com/insights-center/viewpoints/2231/2020-06-18-abbvies-enforcement-its-patent-thicket-humira-under)*

### 6. Gleevec / imatinib — chronic myeloid leukemia

**The gap.** Novartis's Gleevec, a landmark targeted cancer therapy, cost roughly $70,000/year in the US. As the original compound patent neared expiry, Novartis sought a new Indian patent on a specific beta-crystalline *form* of the same molecule — a textbook evergreening attempt.

**Existing patent-free alternative — and a landmark legal precedent.** India's Supreme Court rejected the application in its 2013 *Novartis v. Union of India* ruling, applying Section 3(d) of India's Patents Act, which specifically bars patents on new forms of known substances absent proven enhanced efficacy. The court found Novartis hadn't demonstrated any therapeutic improvement over the original compound — only better bioavailability, which didn't qualify. The result: generic imatinib remained available in India at roughly $2,500/year, against ~$70,000/year for the branded US product. The case is now one of the most-cited global precedents for anti-evergreening patent law, and generic imatinib is now widely available following the compound patent's ordinary expiration in most markets, including the US.

**Status: Resolved**, and notable as a case where a legal system successfully blocked an evergreening attempt before it happened, rather than a workaround after the fact.

*Sources: [Novartis v. Union of India, Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novartis_v._Union_of_India_and_Others), [Legal Service India case analysis](https://www.legalserviceindia.com/Legal-Articles/novartis-ag-v-union-of-india-section-3d-patent-evergreening-judgment/)*

### 7. Copaxone (glatiramer acetate) — multiple sclerosis

**The gap.** As Teva's original Copaxone 20mg-daily patent approached its 2014–2015 expiry, Teva won FDA approval for a new 40mg, three-times-weekly formulation and — per findings later published by the European Commission — ran an internal "Copaxone Continuation Project" explicitly designed to migrate roughly two-thirds of US patients onto the new dosage before generics for the *old* dosage could gain traction, since those generics weren't automatically substitutable for the new one. In November 2024, the European Commission fined Teva €462.6 million for this and related anticompetitive conduct across several drugs.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Sandoz's Glatopa (glatiramer acetate) launched in 2015 for the 20mg dose; after a federal appeals court invalidated Teva's 40mg patents in 2017, Mylan and others launched generics for that dose too in 2017–2018.

**Status: Resolved**, though the EU fine — decided in late 2024, one of the most recent enforcement actions in this whole list — shows regulators are still actively penalizing the "product hopping" tactic years after the fact.

*Sources: [McDermott Will & Emery](https://www.mcdermottlaw.com/insights/european-commission-fines-teva-e462-6-million-for-misusing-divisional-patents-and-disparaging-generic-competitors-in-the-copaxone-market/), [Multiple Sclerosis News Today](https://multiplesclerosisnewstoday.com/news-posts/2024/11/05/ec-slaps-teva-hefty-fine-targeted-glatiramer-acetate-rivals/)*

### 8. Restasis (cyclosporine) — chronic dry eye

**The gap.** Facing patent-invalidity challenges at the US Patent Office, Allergan transferred its Restasis patents to the Saint Regis Mohawk Tribe in September 2017 for $13.75 million upfront plus $15 million/year in royalties, then licensed them back — betting the tribe's sovereign immunity would shield the patents from further review. Both the Federal Circuit (2018) and, on appeal, the Supreme Court (2019, declining to hear the case) rejected the maneuver, ruling tribal sovereign immunity doesn't extend to Patent Office review proceedings.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Generic cyclosporine ophthalmic emulsion is now approved and available following the collapse of the immunity shield and the underlying patents' expiration/invalidation.

**Status: Resolved** — and notable as one of the more brazen documented attempts to use a legal loophole unrelated to drug science to block generic entry, ultimately defeated in court.

*Sources: [Wikipedia case summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_St._Regis_Mohawk_Tribe_and_Restasis_patent), [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/09/08/allergan-does-patent-deal-for-restasis-with-native-american-tribe.html)*

### 9. Colchicine / Colcrys — gout

**The gap.** Colchicine is a natural-product drug used since antiquity, unpatentable in its original form, selling for about a dime a tablet for decades as an "unapproved" but widely used generic. Under the FDA's Unapproved Drugs Initiative — a genuinely well-intentioned program meant to formally validate the safety of pre-1962 drugs still on the market — URL Pharma ran a small clinical trial, won formal FDA approval for a branded version (Colcrys) in 2009, and received three years of market exclusivity plus new patents extending to 2029. As required by the program, the FDA then pulled all the older unapproved (and far cheaper) colchicine products from the market. Price jumped from about $0.09–0.10/tablet to roughly $4.50–5/tablet — one UCLA-led study found a 16-fold increase in average prescription cost between 2008 and 2011, associated with a measurable drop in colchicine use and worse gout control among patients who stopped taking it.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Once Colcrys's exclusivity period and associated patent litigation resolved, authorized and independent generics entered starting around 2015. HHS formally ended the Unapproved Drugs Initiative program in November 2020, citing exactly this kind of outcome.

**Status: Partially resolved.** Generics exist, but prices never fully returned to pre-2009 levels, and the program that enabled this pattern — used on several other old drugs too — has since been discontinued.

*Sources: [The Rheumatologist](https://www.the-rheumatologist.org/article/the-unapproved-drugs-initiative-is-coming-to-an-end/), [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/release/sharp-increase-price-gout-drug-colchicine-led-lower-use-and), [KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/fda-approval/)*

### 10. Albuterol inhalers — asthma, COPD

**The gap.** Albuterol (salbutamol) is an old, cheap, off-patent drug on the WHO Essential Medicines List. What's patented is the *inhaler device*. When the Montreal Protocol forced manufacturers off ozone-depleting CFC propellants and onto HFA propellants, companies patented the new devices — meaning a chemically unchanged, decades-off-patent drug got a fresh multi-year period of device-based exclusivity. Average US retail price today runs around $98 for a generic HFA inhaler without insurance (as low as ~$42 with a coupon), for a drug whose active ingredient costs pennies.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** The first true generic HFA inhaler was approved in 2020; GSK also launched an authorized generic in 2019 at under $36 wholesale. Nebulized albuterol solution — delivered via a separate, older, non-patent-encumbered device — remains a cheaper if less portable option.

**Status: Partially resolved.** Generic competition has arrived, but roughly a decade passed between the CFC phase-out completing (2008) and meaningful generic inhaler competition (2019–2020).

*Sources: [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/asthma-meds-have-become-shockingly-unaffordable-but-relief-may-be-on-the-way-222858), [GoodRx](https://www.goodrx.com/albuterol/albuterol-inhaler-without-insurance)*

### 11. Truvada / Descovy (PrEP) — HIV prevention

**The gap.** Truvada's HIV-prevention use was discovered through CDC-funded research; the CDC subsequently patented that specific use and offered Gilead a license in exchange for royalties. Gilead refused, and in 2019 the US government sued Gilead for patent infringement — an unusual case of the federal government suing a drugmaker over the government's *own* patents on publicly-funded research the company was profiting from (Truvada generated roughly $3 billion in 2018 sales alone). Gilead won at trial in 2023 (jury found the patents invalid/not infringed); the government appealed; the two sides settled in early 2025, with terms undisclosed.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Teva launched generic tenofovir/emtricitabine in the US in October 2020 at a wholesale price only modestly below brand ($48.51/tablet vs. Gilead's ~$58), meaning limited savings at first from a single generic manufacturer. That has changed substantially since: as of 2026, GoodRx lists generic emtricitabine/tenofovir at roughly $26–60/month with a standard coupon — a genuinely resolved outcome, down from list prices around $1,600–2,200/month for the brand.

**Status: Resolved**, following a slower-than-expected but ultimately real transition to cheap generics, plus a 2025 legal resolution of the underlying government-patent dispute.

*Sources: [Fierce Pharma](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/gilead-settles-us-government-long-running-patent-dust-over-truvada-and-descovy), [GoodRx (2026 pricing)](https://www.goodrx.com/emtricitabine-tenofovir), [HIV.gov](https://www.hiv.gov/blog/united-states-files-patent-infringement-lawsuit-against-gilead-related-truvada-and-descovy-pre)*

### 12. CAR-T cell therapies — certain blood cancers

**The gap.** Commercial CAR-T products (Kymriah, Yescarta, and others) list between roughly $373,000 and $605,000 per one-time infusion, with total treatment costs — including hospitalization for side-effect management — frequently exceeding $500,000–$1 million. The manufacturing process (extracting a patient's T-cells, genetically engineering them, expanding them, reinfusing them) is patent- and know-how-protected by the commercial developers.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** A genuinely promising, actively growing model: academic and nonprofit "point-of-care" manufacturing. Hospital Clínic de Barcelona developed its own CAR-T product (ARI-0001), reimbursed by Spain's health ministry at about €89,000–97,000 — roughly a fifth of commercial pricing. Case Western Reserve/Ohio State's academic program reports manufacturing costs around $27,000. The nonprofit Caring Cross has built a point-of-care model estimated at $20,000 in materials plus $10,000 in labor, now being deployed with Brazil's Oswaldo Cruz Foundation. India's ImmunoACT produces a homegrown CAR-T therapy (NexCAR19) for roughly $20,000–40,000 — about a tenth of US pricing — and a 2025 Indian clinical trial (VELCART) reported a median total treatment cost of about $12,700.

**Status: Emerging, not yet at scale.** These programs are real, published, and clinically validated, but remain limited to a small number of pioneering centers rather than being broadly available the way generic drugs are.

*Sources: [MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2024/04/12/1091161/car-t-cancer-therapy-treatment-expense/), [Blood Advances (ASH)](https://ashpublications.org/bloodadvances/article/8/23/6133/517768/Point-of-care-CAR-T-manufacturing-solutions-can-1), [BioInformant](https://bioinformant.com/ways-to-curb-the-sky-high-costs-of-car-t-cell-therapy/)*

### 13. Trikafta — cystic fibrosis

**The gap.** Vertex's Trikafta (elexacaftor/ivacaftor/tezacaftor) treats the underlying cause of cystic fibrosis in roughly 90% of patients, at a US list price exceeding $300,000/year ($11 billion in 2024 sales). Vertex has, for years, declined to register the drug for sale in many countries — including South Africa — while still holding patent rights there, meaning the *only* legal path to the medicine was import at full US pricing. A 2023 South African lawsuit (Cheri Nel v. Vertex) sought a first-ever compulsory license to allow generic import; during the case, patients could only access a generic version (Trixacar, ~$60,000/year) by traveling to Argentina in person. Under public pressure, Vertex began allowing limited South African imports via a special-exemption mechanism in 2024, and the lawsuit was dropped — but as of an August 2025 patient-advocacy filing, only about half of the roughly 420 eligible South African patients actually have access, and advocates are pushing to reopen a competition-law investigation into Vertex.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** A patient "buyers' club" announced in October 2025 that Bangladeshi manufacturer Beximco will launch a generic version ("Triko") in spring 2026, priced at $12,750/year for adults — Bangladesh, as a UN-designated least-developed country, isn't bound by WTO pharmaceutical patent rules until 2033.

**Status: Unresolved for most patients globally**, though the 2026 Bangladeshi generic and continued legal/advocacy pressure represent genuine, if partial and recent, progress.

*Sources: [Fierce Pharma](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/vertex-under-fire-cystic-fibrosis-buyers-club-resurfaces-plan-debut-cheaper-trikafta-generic), [Spotlight (South Africa)](https://www.spotlightnsp.co.za/2024/08/06/case-against-vertex-dropped-after-cystic-fibrosis-medicine-price-reduced/), [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/pharmalot/2025/08/08/vertex-cystic-fibrosis-access-medicines-pharma-south-africa-patents-trikafta/)*

### 14. Zolgensma / Itvisma — spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)

**The gap.** Novartis's Zolgensma, a one-time AAV gene therapy, launched in 2019 at $2.125 million — then the most expensive drug ever priced — and its 2025 intrathecal successor Itvisma launched at $2.59 million. A 2025 ProPublica investigation traced the therapy's roots to over $450 million in NIH funding plus grassroots family fundraising, with the resulting commercial rights ultimately concentrated in a single company after an $8.7 billion acquisition. More than 5,000 patients have been treated globally since 2019, generating over $6.4 billion in cumulative Novartis revenue — but the therapy remains functionally unavailable to most families in low- and middle-income countries, including reports of struggling families in India and South Africa.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** None. This is a genuine, current gap: AAV gene-therapy manufacturing is technically demanding enough (specialized viral-vector production, strict quality control) that no academic or nonprofit point-of-care model comparable to the CAR-T examples above yet exists for this class of therapy. Chronic alternatives exist (Spinraza, Evrysdi) but are themselves expensive, branded, ongoing treatments rather than patent-free substitutes.

**Status: Unresolved.** Worth naming plainly, since it's the clearest case in this whole document where no legitimate lower-cost pathway currently exists.

*Sources: [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/zolgensma-sma-novartis-drug-prices-gene-therapy-avexis), [Fierce Pharma](https://www.fiercepharma.com/pharma/novartis-scores-fda-approval-new-version-sma-gene-therapy-prices-259m)*

### 15. Naloxone / Narcan — opioid overdose reversal

**The gap.** Naloxone itself is old and cheap; Narcan's nasal-spray delivery format carried device-related exclusivity for years. When generic nasal naloxone was finally approved in 2021, analysts had expected a 20–30% discount — it launched only about 5% cheaper than brand Narcan.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** The FDA approved Narcan for over-the-counter sale in March 2023 (retailing around $45/two-dose box) and a nonprofit-made generic, RiVive (from Harm Reduction Therapeutics), followed. As of 2026, GoodRx lists a coupon price around $41 against an average retail price of $118 — real savings, though advocates note even $41–45 remains a meaningful barrier for the population most affected by opioid use disorder, who are disproportionately low-income.

**Status: Largely resolved on legal/regulatory access (no prescription needed anywhere in the US); only partially resolved on price**, which is why many states and nonprofits still separately fund free distribution programs rather than relying on retail pricing.

*Sources: [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/08/30/1196874196/over-the-counter-narcan-may-be-too-expensive-for-some-people-advocates-fear), [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2023/04/17/otc-narcan-naloxone-price-availability/), [GoodRx (2026)](https://www.goodrx.com/narcan)*

### 16. Semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) — diabetes and obesity

**The gap.** This is the most current case in the list and still actively unfolding. Novo Nordisk's semaglutide drugs generated roughly $26 billion in 2024 revenue. The core compound patent (filed 2006) is beginning its ordinary 20-year expiration in several countries in 2026 — including India, Canada, China, Brazil, and Turkey, representing about 40% of the world's population — but not in the US, where patent-term extensions and a stack of 15+ additional formulation- and device-related patents in the FDA's Orange Book push realistic generic/biosimilar entry to the early-to-mid 2030s. US list prices run $935–1,349+/month.

**Existing patent-free alternative.** Generic semaglutide is already selling in India — in some cases at prices up to 92% below the branded product — with several more large markets following through 2026 as their patents lapse. In the US specifically, there has been a real (if narrowing) domestic workaround: FDA drug-shortage rules allowed compounding pharmacies to sell compounded semaglutide for roughly $99–299/month starting in 2022. The FDA formally ended the shortage designation in February 2025, and by 2026 compounding is restricted to patients with a specific, documented medical need (such as an allergy to a brand ingredient) rather than being broadly available as a cost workaround. Separately, in November 2025 the US government negotiated Medicare/Medicaid pricing down to roughly $245–274/month (about a 70% cut from list) via a mix of Inflation Reduction Act price negotiation and a direct deal with Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly — a policy-driven price cut that leaves the underlying patents fully intact.

**Status: Unresolved in the US** until the underlying patents lapse in the early-to-mid 2030s, though genuinely resolved already in India and soon several other large markets, and partially mitigated in the US via negotiated pricing rather than generic competition.

*Sources: [medRxiv production-cost analysis](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.04.26347508v1.full), [IQVIA](https://www.iqvia.com/locations/emea/blogs/2025/07/off-patent-semaglutide), [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=ad05113a-d1ea-4ac9-ae11-00c2c0e87f31)*

## Part 2 — Structural pathways and initiatives already working on this

These aren't case-specific fixes — they're the legal, nonprofit, and grassroots mechanisms that produced several of the "resolved" outcomes above, and that continue operating on new cases.

### Medicines Patent Pool (MPP)
UN-backed (founded by Unitaid in 2010), MPP negotiates voluntary licenses from patent-holding companies and grants non-exclusive sub-licenses to generic manufacturers for use in low- and middle-income countries, with royalties flowing back to the original patent holder. It's the mechanism behind the tenofovir and dolutegravir generics that took the annual cost of HIV treatment in Africa from roughly $10,000 to under $70. A Lancet Public Health model projected the 2014 dolutegravir license alone would increase treatment uptake by 15.5 million patient-years and avert over 150,000 deaths by 2032. MPP has since expanded beyond HIV/Hepatitis C/TB into COVID-19 therapeutics (including a 2021 Pfizer license for Paxlovid covering 95 countries) and other essential medicines.
*[medicinespatentpool.org](https://medicinespatentpool.org/)*

### Drugs for Neglected Diseases initiative (DNDi)
A nonprofit founded in 2003 by Médecins Sans Frontières (funded partly with MSF's Nobel Peace Prize money) plus public partners, explicitly built as a not-for-profit alternative drug-development model. It has produced 13 new treatments across six diseases since founding, most notably fexinidazole — the first entirely oral cure for sleeping sickness, approved in the DRC in 2018 — developed through public/philanthropic funding (Gates Foundation, Wellcome Trust, Unitaid) rather than a patent-monopoly revenue model.
*[dndi.org](https://dndi.org/)*

### Open-source and crowdsourced drug discovery
A loose but real ecosystem: India's government-run Open Source Drug Discovery project (2008–2017) used a wiki-based platform for neglected-disease research under the motto "affordable healthcare for all." The Open Source Pharma Foundation (Paris, founded 2018, Tata Trusts-funded) advocates for and facilitates open, patent-free pharma R&D. Most notably, the 2020 COVID Moonshot project crowdsourced roughly 18,000 antiviral compound designs from over 200 volunteer chemists across 25 countries via open call, synthesizing and testing 2,400 of them; it evolved into the well-funded ASAP Discovery consortium (a $65 million NIH grant, now developing pan-coronavirus antivirals). M4K Pharma is piloting a related model — using regulatory exclusivity rather than patents as its only commercial protection — for a rare pediatric brain tumor.
*[Open Source Pharma Foundation](https://www.ospfound.org/about.html), [ASAP Discovery background](https://www.blopig.com/blog/2025/05/open-source-pharma-from-idealism-to-pragmatic-solutions/)*

### Open Insulin Project
Started in 2015 by a Bay Area biohacker community (Counter Culture Labs), now with volunteer chapters in Baltimore, Paris, Brazil, Senegal, and Cameroon, working toward a fully open-source insulin biosynthesis protocol plus open-hardware bioreactors and purification equipment. Worth being honest about where it actually stands: a 2025 academic case study of the project found that the US regulatory environment — not patent exclusivity alone — is the larger remaining barrier, and that purification, scaling, and formal approval remain unresolved even with an open protocol in hand. It's a genuine and values-driven effort, but has not yet produced a deployable medical-grade product.
*[openinsulin.org](https://openinsulin.org/), [academic case study, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30220578/)*

### Four Thieves Vinegar Collective — a cautionary edge case, not a recommendation
Worth including because it's real and well-documented in mainstream press (Wired, Vice, NPR-affiliate KQED), but I'm deliberately not describing their methods. This is an anarchist biohacker collective that publishes open-source hardware designs (a DIY chemical-reaction device) and instructions intended to let individuals synthesize certain medicines at home, motivated by cases exactly like EpiPen and Daraprim above. Take this as a marker of how far the "open pharma" spectrum extends, not as a source of guidance: researchers at MIT and elsewhere have publicly warned that unsupervised home synthesis without professional quality control risks producing the wrong drug, the wrong dose, or harmful byproducts, and the FDA has issued a public warning specifically about this group's work. Right-to-repair-for-medicine as an idea is one thing; unregulated personal drug manufacturing carries real, serious safety risk that the licensed pathways above don't.
*[Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_Thieves_Vinegar_Collective)*

### TRIPS flexibilities, the Doha Declaration, and compulsory licensing
The WTO's 1994 TRIPS Agreement set global minimum patent standards, including for medicines — and directly threatened the generic industries (especially India's) that were then supplying most of the developing world's medicine. The 2001 Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health affirmed countries' right to override patents for public-health reasons via compulsory licensing, with a 2003/2005 follow-up amendment specifically enabling licenses for *export* to countries lacking their own manufacturing capacity. The most consequential real-world test of this came in South Africa: in 1998, the Pharmaceutical Manufacturers' Association and 39 multinational drug companies sued the South African government over its 1997 Medicines Act, which allowed generic substitution and parallel importing to fight the HIV/AIDS crisis. The Treatment Action Campaign joined as amicus curiae; under sustained global pressure — a 285,000-signature petition from 140 organizations in 130 countries — the companies unconditionally dropped the case in April 2001, clearing the way for the generic antiretroviral scale-up that followed across the continent.
*[Doha Declaration, WTO](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/trips_e/pharmpatent_e.htm), [MSF Access Campaign](https://msfaccess.org/south-africa-big-pharma-backs-down)*

### The biosimilar regulatory pathway
The US Biologics Price Competition and Innovation Act (2010) created an abbreviated approval pathway for biosimilars, analogous to small-molecule generics but accounting for the fact that biologics can't be exactly replicated. The results, per the 2025 industry/IQVIA savings report: generics and biosimilars together saved the US healthcare system $467 billion in 2024 alone ($3.4 trillion over the past decade), with biosimilars specifically contributing $20.2 billion in 2024 and $56.2 billion cumulatively since the first approval in 2015. The same report flags a real structural risk going forward: of 118 biologics losing patent exclusivity over the next decade, only 12 currently have a biosimilar in development — a "biosimilar void" that could leave a projected $234 billion in savings unrealized if the pipeline doesn't fill in.
*[AAM/Biosimilars Council 2025 Savings Report](https://accessiblemeds.org/resources/reports/2025-savings-report/)*

### Bayh-Dole march-in rights (an unused tool)
The 1980 Bayh-Dole Act lets federal agencies "march in" and force relicensing of a patent on an invention that was developed with federal funding, if it isn't being made available on reasonable terms. In 45 years, it has never once been exercised — the NIH has received and denied eight petitions, including ones concerning Norvir/ritonavir and Xalatan/latanoprost (2004), Fabrazyme, and Xtandi. The law's original co-authors, Senators Bayh and Dole, stated in 2002 that they never intended it as a price-control mechanism. A December 2023 draft federal framework proposed, for the first time, allowing a drug's price to be considered as one factor in a march-in decision — a live, unresolved policy debate as of 2026, not yet used.
*[CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/march-rights-and-us-global-competitiveness), [Goodwin Law](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2023/12/alerts-lifesciences-federal-government-releases-proposed-guidance)*

### Universities Allied for Essential Medicines (UAEM)
A student-led advocacy network (founded 2001, active on 100+ campuses in 20+ countries) that targets a specific structural gap: universities are frequently the *original* patent holders on early-stage, publicly-funded drug research, and it's the terms of the university's license to a pharma company — not just the pharma company's own conduct — that shape eventual pricing and access. UAEM's Global Access Licensing Framework has been adopted, in whole or modified form, by UCLA (developed with the Medicines Patent Pool), UC Berkeley, Columbia, and McGill, among others, requiring licensees to commit to affordable-access plans in low- and middle-income countries as a condition of the license.
*[Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universities_Allied_for_Essential_Medicines), [UAEM white paper](https://www.uaem.org/tools/early-action-for-equitable-access)*

## Part 3 — Where no good alternative exists yet (the honest gap list)

Being direct about this, since it was explicitly part of the ask:

- **AAV/lentiviral gene therapies broadly** (Zolgensma/Itvisma for SMA, and the same dynamic applies to gene therapies for hemophilia and to CRISPR-based Casgevy for sickle cell, priced around $2.2 million) — manufacturing complexity puts these outside what any current academic or nonprofit model has managed to replicate at accessible cost, unlike the CAR-T case above.
- **Trikafta and CFTR modulators**, for the large majority of cystic fibrosis patients outside the US, UK, and a few other wealthy markets — real progress via litigation, importation programs, and the 2026 Bangladeshi generic, but not yet a stable, broadly available generic pathway.
- **CAR-T and other advanced cell therapies**, for patients outside a small number of pioneering academic centers in Spain, India, Brazil, and a handful of US institutions — the point-of-care model works, but hasn't scaled.
- **Semaglutide and other GLP-1 drugs, specifically in the US** — a functioning cheap generic already exists in other countries right now; in the US it's a matter of waiting out a 2030s patent estate, with compounding-pharmacy and cross-border workarounds actively narrowing rather than expanding as of 2026.
