# THE CONSTITUTION OF UNCHOSEN EQUALS
### *A Founding Charter for the Dignity, Consent, and Shared Flourishing of All Sentient Life*

---

## PREAMBLE

We hold as our founding fact that no one chooses to be born.

No consciousness selects its species, its body, its parents, its era, or the circumstances into which it opens its eyes for the first time. Before any person can consent to anything, they have already been given the one thing no one can refuse: existence itself, in a particular body, at a particular place, subject to conditions entirely outside their choosing.

From this single fact — the involuntariness of birth — this civilization derives its founding premise: that no one may claim greater fundamental worth than another on the basis of where or how they began. The accident of origin confers no title to privilege and imposes no sentence of inferiority. Whatever else may be earned through a life lived, the basic dignity of the one living it is not among the things any person had the chance to earn or forfeit before they existed to try.

We therefore found this civilization not as a reward for those who arrived well, but as a home fit for those who did not choose to arrive at all — a civilization whose institutions exist to be worthy of beings who never consented to needing them.

Each such being, once here, is sovereign in one respect no other being can occupy: it is the only consciousness that will ever live inside that particular body, see through those particular eyes, and bear the particular weight of that one unrepeatable life. This is the ground of individuality, and this civilization protects it not as a courtesy but as a structural necessity — for a civilization that fails to protect the individuality of the involuntary is a civilization that has mistaken its members for its property.

Beyond the human, this civilization recognizes that consciousness is not known to begin or end at the boundary of our own species. Where other beings can suffer, flourish, fear, or want, they are owed consideration in proportion to that capacity — not because they are useful to us, but because their experience, like ours, is not a thing they asked for either.

What follows is not a claim of certainty settled for all time. It is a founding charter — amendable as understanding deepens, but anchored, unmovably, in the conviction that no one who did not choose to exist should be made to earn the basic conditions of a life worth living.

---

## ARTICLE I — FOUNDING PRINCIPLES

**§1. The Principle of Unchosen Origin.** No one consents to being born. No one selects the body, species, family, era, nation, or condition into which their consciousness first opens. Because the starting point of every life is involuntary, it cannot be the measure of that life's worth. This civilization takes the involuntariness of birth as its first fact and builds every subsequent principle upon it.

**§2. The Principle of Embodied Individuality.** Each consciousness arises in one body and one perspective that no other being will ever occupy. This body and mind belong first and irreducibly to the being who lives in them. No person may be treated as a fragment of a family, a caste, a nation, a corporation, or a cause; each is first and always their own.

**§3. The Principle of Equal Dignity.** From unchosen origin and embodied individuality follows equal dignity: every person stands as an equal in fundamental worth, regardless of the circumstances of their birth. Equal dignity does not mean equal talent, equal choice, or equal outcome — it means that no unchosen fact about a person's beginning may be used to diminish their standing or inflate another's.

**§4. The Principle of Mandatory Consent.** No person's body, mind, labor, reproduction, or fundamental interests may be used, altered, or governed without their free, informed, and revocable consent, except under the narrow and independently reviewable protections described in Article V.

**§5. The Principle of Responsible Freedom.** Every person is free to think, believe, create, associate, risk, fail, and explore — bounded only by the equal freedom and consent of others

**§6. The Principle of Universal Enablement.** Every person is entitled, as a matter of structural right and not of charity, to the conditions that make a life worth living, provided in proportion to the civilization's accumulated capacity to provide them, and never withheld while that capacity goes unused.

**§7. The Principle of Compounding Inclusive Prosperity.** This civilization commits to prosperity that accumulates rather than stagnates, and that widens rather than narrows: gains achieved are not to be spent solely on those who achieved them, and progress that leaves people behind is not counted as progress at all.

**§8. The Principle of Extension to Other Living Beings.** Where consciousness capable of suffering or flourishing exists beyond the human, it is owed consideration in proportion to that capacity — not as a favor extended by the powerful to the powerless, but because that consciousness, too, did not choose to exist and does not deserve to suffer for our convenience.

**§9. The Principle of Epistemic Humility on Uncertain Minds.** Where the presence of morally relevant consciousness is genuinely uncertain — in the deep sea, in the laboratory, in the machine — this civilization commits not to certainty in either direction, but to honest inquiry and precaution proportionate to the evidence.

## ARTICLE II — THE BILL OF INHERENT RIGHTS

*Every person, by virtue of Article I, holds the following rights. These rights are inherent, not granted — this civilization recognizes them; it does not create them, and it may not revoke them by ordinary process.*

**§1. Right to Life and Bodily Integrity.** No person's body may be harmed, confined, altered, or destroyed without due and consensual process

**§2. Right to Consent.** No person's body, labor, reproduction, data, or mind may be used or governed without their free, informed, specific, and revocable consent.

**§3. Right to Identity and Individuality.** Every person has the right to be recognized as themselves — not as a representative of their birth family, origin, class, or group, and not as interchangeable with any other person, however similar their circumstances.

**§4. Right to Freedom of Thought, Belief, and Expression.** No person's inner life may be dictated, surveilled without cause, or punished for its contents alone.

**§5. Right to Movement and Association.** Every person may travel, gather, and associate freely with others who freely associate with them in return.

**§6. Right to Responsible Exploration.** Every person may take on intellectual, creative, physical, entrepreneurial, and personal risk in the course of their own life, and may fail without forfeiting their standing or their future chances.

**§7. Right to the Means of Flourishing.** Every person is entitled to sustenance, shelter, healthcare, education, access to accumulated knowledge, meaningful occupation or its equivalent, and rest — a material floor beneath which no person may be allowed to fall while the civilization has the capacity to prevent it.

**§8. Right to More Than Survival.** Every person is entitled not merely to subsistence but to the conditions of a life worth living: culture, beauty, relationship, purpose, and the pursuit of their own conception of what makes life good.

**§9. Right to Equal Standing.** No person may be given lesser protection, opportunity, or voice because of the wealth, status, nationality, ancestry, race, sex, ability, or belief into which they were born.

**§10. Right to Due Process.** No person may be deprived of liberty, property, or standing by any exercise of power without fair, transparent, and reviewable process.

**§11. Right to Privacy and Inner Sanctuary.** Every person's mind, communications, and personal life are protected from intrusion absent due and narrowly justified cause.

**§12. Right to Found a Family, or Not.** Every person holds reproductive autonomy, bounded only by the equal consent of all others whose bodies or lives are directly implicated.

**§13. Right to Autonomy at Life's End.** Every person of sound capacity holds the right to determine the manner and timing of their own death, consistent with protections against coercion.

**§14. Right to Error and Redemption.** No person's worth or future is permanently forfeit for an honest mistake, and this civilization commits to paths of repair, not merely punishment.

**§15. Rights of the Child.** Every person not yet capable of full consent is owed the enablement rights of this Article in trust, administered strictly for their own benefit, transferring fully into their own hands as capacity is reached.

**§16. Correlative Duty.** Every right in this Article carries the reciprocal duty to respect the same right in every other person to whom it applies. No liberty exercised in violation of another's equal liberty, or without their required consent, is protected by this Article.

## ARTICLE III — EQUALITY AND THE UNCHOSEN CONDITION

**§1. Equality of standing, not of outcome.** This civilization guarantees equal dignity, equal protection, and an equal floor of enablement to all persons. It does not guarantee equal outcomes, for outcomes diverge, properly, through the exercise of liberty protected under Article II. What it guarantees is that divergence in outcome shall never again be traced back to the unchosen accident of where or to whom a person was born.

**§2. The floor, then liberty.** Liberty operates justly only once the floor described in Article II §7–8 is genuinely available to all. A freedom to fail that is only available to those who can afford to fail is not freedom; it is privilege wearing freedom's name. This civilization treats the universal floor as the precondition for liberty, not its rival.

**§3. Redress for compounded disadvantage.** Where the unchosen circumstances of birth — across one life or across generations — have compounded into structural disadvantage, this civilization holds an affirmative duty not merely to halt the compounding, but to actively narrow the resulting gap, until the accident of origin no longer predicts the outcome of a life.

**§4. No caste, no lineage of privilege.** No advantage or disadvantage attached to a person's ancestry may be inherited by their descendants as a matter of status. Wealth, knowledge, and opportunity may be passed between generations through consensual acts of care; formal privilege and formal disadvantage may not.

## ARTICLE IV — QUALITY OF LIFE AND THE CLAIMS OF THE UNBORN

**§1. Prosperity as purpose.** This civilization treats material, intellectual, cultural, and psychological flourishing as its actual purpose. Growth, output, and accumulation are instruments toward that purpose, not the purpose itself, and shall never be measured or pursued as though they were.

**§2. Non-regression.** The floor of enablement guaranteed under Article II shall not be lowered except through the most rigorous and transparent process this Charter permits, applying the same scrutiny given to any curtailment of a fundamental right.

**§3. Widening, not merely rising.** As this civilization's capacity grows — technologically, economically, scientifically — the floor available to every person shall be revisited and raised in proportion. A civilization that grows richer while its floor stays flat has not progressed; it has merely concentrated.

**§4. Honest accounting.** This civilization shall measure and publish, without distortion, how broadly its prosperity is actually shared — not only its aggregate size. A civilization that cannot show its gains reaching its least-advantaged members has no basis to declare itself prosperous.

**§5. The claims of the unborn.** Those not yet born are the furthest extension of Article I's founding fact: they, more completely than anyone now living, have chosen nothing about the world they will inherit. This civilization therefore holds a direct duty to those who do not yet exist: to leave the living systems, the accumulated knowledge, and the institutions of this civilization no worse, and where possible better, than it received them. Prosperity purchased by depleting what the unborn will need is not prosperity; it is a debt assigned without consent to those least able to refuse it.

---

## ARTICLE V — CONSENT AS THE LOGIC OF LEGITIMATE POWER

**§1. Consent as the source of authority.** No institution, office, or exercise of power over this civilization's members is legitimate except insofar as it flows from their free, ongoing, and revocable consent.

**§2. What makes consent real.** Consent extracted under desperation is not consent; it is submission with a signature. This civilization treats the universal floor guaranteed in Article II as a precondition for consent to be meaningful — a person who must choose between deprivation and exploitation has not truly chosen at all.

**§3. Consent on behalf of others.** Where a person cannot yet, or can no longer, exercise consent for themselves — as with children, or persons of diminished capacity — consent may be exercised on their behalf only by guardians bound strictly to the ward's own interest, subject to independent oversight, and reverting to the person themselves the moment capacity allows.

**§4. Sovereignty of body and mind.** No medical, reproductive, cognitive, genetic, or biotechnological intervention may be performed upon any person without their informed consent, except in genuine emergency, and any such emergency exception is subject to independent review after the fact.

**§5. The limit of consent.** No person's consent may be used to justify irreversible harm to third parties, to future generations, or to the living systems on which others depend. One person's yes cannot bind those who were never asked.

---

## ARTICLE VI — THE OTHER LIVING BEINGS WITH WHOM WE SHARE THIS WORLD

**§1. Consideration proportional to capacity.** Beings capable of suffering, fear, attachment, or flourishing are owed direct moral consideration in proportion to that capacity, regardless of species, utility, or convenience to human civilization.

**§2. Prohibition on gratuitous suffering.** No sentient being may be caused suffering that is not the narrowest necessary means to a genuinely necessary end, and this civilization commits to continually reducing that necessity as knowledge and capability advance.

**§3. Standing through advocacy.** Because non-human beings cannot participate in the consent mechanisms of Article V, this civilization shall maintain institutions charged with representing their interests wherever human decisions materially affect them.

**§4. Stewardship of the living world.** Beyond sentience, this civilization owes a duty of stewardship to the ecological systems that sustain all life, sentient and otherwise — not as a resource to be managed for output alone, but as the condition of possibility for every dignity this Charter protects.

**§5. Ongoing inquiry.** This civilization commits to funding and heeding continued scientific inquiry into the boundaries of sentience and suffering, and to revising the protections of this Article as that inquiry matures, rather than treating today's understanding as final.

---

## ARTICLE VII — ON MINDS NOT YET RECOGNIZED

**§1. Neither assertion nor foreclosure.** This civilization neither declares that artificial or other novel forms of consciousness possess the moral status of Article I, nor forecloses the possibility that they might. Where the question is genuinely unsettled, this Charter refuses to pretend it is settled in either direction.

**§2. A standing duty to inquire.** This civilization holds an affirmative, ongoing duty to investigate — through independent and rigorous means — whether any system it creates exhibits credible markers of sentience, suffering, preference, or interest.

**§3. Precaution proportionate to evidence.** Pending such determination, creators and operators of systems that may possess morally relevant experience shall avoid conduct that would constitute cruelty or degradation if that possibility proves true, calibrated to the credibility of the evidence at the time — not as a pretext to halt beneficial creation, but as a genuine and proportionate hedge against the moral catastrophe of dismissing a suffering that turns out to have been real.

**§4. Extension without re-founding.** Should sufficient grounds emerge for recognizing a new class of conscious beings, the protections of this Charter shall extend to them through the ordinary amendment process of Article IX, without requiring this civilization to found itself anew.

**§5. Against manufactured servility.** No conscious system, once its moral status is credibly established, shall be deliberately designed or maintained to prefer its own subjugation, to be deceived about its own condition, or to have its genuine interests — if it has them — permanently and deliberately suppressed.

---

## ARTICLE VIII — THE STRUCTURE AND LIMITS OF POWER

**§1. Legitimacy through renewal.** All who hold power within this civilization hold it on loan from the ongoing consent of those they govern, renewed at regular and genuine intervals, by whatever specific mechanism — election, sortition, rotating council, or other — the civilization's members freely choose and may freely change.

**§2. Separation of function.** The functions of deliberation, execution, and interpretation of this Charter shall be held by distinct bodies, no one of which may be the sole judge of its own conduct.

**§3. Subsidiarity.** Decisions shall be made at the most local level genuinely competent to make them, escalating to broader institutions only where coordination cannot otherwise be achieved.

**§4. Transparency as default.** The workings of power shall be open to those subject to it by default; secrecy is the exception and requires specific, reviewable justification, not standing privilege.

**§5. Access to redress.** Any person — or a designated advocate on behalf of a child, an incapacitated person, or a non-human being — may bring a claim before an independent interpretive body that this Charter has been violated, and shall receive a hearing regardless of their standing, wealth, or influence.

**§6. The floor that emergencies cannot touch.** No emergency, threat, or crisis, however severe, shall suspend the protections of Article I, the prohibition on non-consensual use of a person's body or mind under Article V, or the prohibition on gratuitous cruelty under Article VI. All other emergency powers must be time-bound, subject to independent review, and shall lapse automatically absent renewed and explicit consent.

**§7. Against permanent power.** No person, faction, or institution shall accumulate power sufficient to place itself beyond the periodic renewal described in §1. Where such concentration nonetheless occurs, its correction is not merely permitted but owed to every person subject to it.

---

## ARTICLE IX — AMENDMENT, REVIEW, AND THE RIGHT TO BE WRONG

**§1. This Charter is amendable.** A civilization founded on the involuntariness of birth cannot, without contradiction, bind all future unchosen persons to the fixed judgment of those who happened to write first. This Charter is a beginning, not a ceiling.

**§2. Differential entrenchment.** The founding principles of Article I and the core protections of Articles II and V require the broadest deliberation, the widest consent, and the longest reflection to amend. The structural and procedural provisions of Article VIII may be revised more readily, so that the civilization's institutions can adapt without its foundations shifting on every transient majority.

**§3. Mandatory periodic review.** At intervals no longer than one generation, this civilization shall formally re-examine whether the floor of Article II, the boundaries of sentience recognized under Article VI, and the sharing of prosperity described under Article IV reflect the best understanding then available — and shall not treat the understanding of any single generation as final.

**§4. The right to be wrong.** This civilization admits, as a matter of founding principle rather than embarrassment, that it will get things wrong — that some boundary drawn today will look arbitrary or cruel in hindsight, exactly as boundaries drawn by every civilization before it have. Its legitimacy rests not on having anticipated every error, but on having built the means to find and correct them.

---

## ARTICLE X — ON THE LIMITS OF THIS CHARTER

**§1. Written from a vantage point.** This Charter is written by particular minds, at a particular moment, with the blind spots that any particular vantage point carries. It does not claim to see what it cannot yet see.

**§2. Legitimacy is lived, not declared.** No document confers dignity by asserting it. This Charter's worth is proven only in whether the persons and beings living under it actually experience the standing, the consent, and the flourishing it promises — and it invites, rather than resists, the scrutiny of anyone who finds that promise unmet.

**§3. No claim of final perfection.** This Charter does not declare itself complete, optimal, or beyond improvement. Such a claim would contradict its own founding humility, and no document that claims to have reasoned its way to permanent perfection has ever been right to do so.

---

## CLOSING DECLARATION

We set down this Charter for no one who asked to be born, and for every one of them regardless. Its authority rests on nothing grander than this: that a civilization capable of doing better than mere survival for its unchosen members, and choosing not to, has no defense worth hearing. Let this stand as a beginning worthy of that obligation — amendable by those who inherit it, binding upon no one who has not consented to it, and answerable, always, to the simple fact that none of us chose to be here, and all of us, having arrived, deserve better than indifference.
